Section 1: Introduction Templates

Paraphrase the question + write a powerful overview. Two paragraphs, zero hesitation.

🎯 The examiner reads your introduction first. It must do TWO jobs: (1) paraphrase the question without copying it, and (2) state the main overall trend or dominant feature. For pie charts, this means identifying the largest/smallest segments and any notable groupings.
🔧 Introduction Template — Plug & Play The pie chart illustrates / depicts / shows the proportion / distribution / breakdown of [topic] in [place/time].
Overall, it is clear that [largest category] accounted for / represented / made up the most significant share, while [smallest category] constituted the smallest proportion.
Band 6 — Safe Start
The pie chart shows the distribution of spending on different modes of transport in a city. Overall, private car use accounted for the largest proportion, while walking represented the smallest share.
Subject Verb Collocation Connector Proportion Language
Band 7 — Strong Paraphrase
The pie chart illustrates the breakdown of expenditure on various transport methods within a single urban area. Overall, it is evident that private vehicles dominated the chart, accounting for well over a third of total spending.
Band 8 — Sophisticated Overview
The chart provides a visual breakdown of how transport expenditure was distributed across four distinct categories in the city. Notably, just two categories — private cars and public transport — collectively accounted for nearly two-thirds of all spending, while the remaining proportion was more evenly divided.
Band 9 — Examiner-Level Precision
As illustrated in the pie chart, transport expenditure was heavily skewed towards motorised travel, with private car use and public transport comprising approximately 65% of the total budget. Conversely, non-motorised modes — namely walking and cycling — constituted the remaining minority share.
🚫 Don't Say These — Common Introduction Mistakes
The pie chart is about transport. → The pie chart illustrates the distribution of transport expenditure.
We can see that cars are the most. → Private cars accounted for the largest proportion.
This pie chart shows the proportions of... → Never copy the question wording — always paraphrase!
The pie chart gives information about... → Too vague — name the specific topic immediately.
Band 7 — Two Pie Charts (Comparative Introduction)
The two pie charts compare the distribution of household income allocation in 1990 and 2020. Overall, housing costs remained the dominant expenditure in both years, though the proportion attributed to leisure and entertainment increased markedly over the period.

Section 2: Key Vocabulary Patterns

Proportion language, fraction expressions, and description verbs that examiners love.

🎯 Pie charts are ALL about proportions. Unlike line graphs (which use trend language), pie charts require precision with fractions, percentages, and relative size. The student who knows five ways to say "the biggest slice" will always outscore the one who repeats "the most."
💡 Secret Weapon for Pie Charts
Master the Proportion Pyramid: start with the largest category, group similar-sized ones together, and always end with a contrast. This structure makes your writing feel organised and analytical — exactly what Band 7+ requires.
🔧 Proportion Language Framework [Subject] accounted for / represented / comprised / constituted / made up + [percentage / fraction / descriptor]

[Percentage] of the total / of all respondents / of the budget was allocated to / attributed to / spent on + [category]
Band 6 — Basic Proportion
Private cars made up 38% of the total transport spending, making it the largest category.
Band 7 — Precise + Varied
Expenditure on private cars accounted for the lion's share of the budget at 38%, followed closely by public transport, which represented just over a quarter of the total.
Band 8 — Fraction Mastery
Private vehicles dominated transport spending, comprising approximately two-fifths of all expenditure, whereas cycling and walking combined constituted little more than a third.
Band 7 — Emphasis with "Only"
Walking, the least popular mode, accounted for a mere 15% of spending — only a fraction of the amount allocated to private car use.
Band 8 — Passive + Proportion
Approximately 65% of the total budget was directed towards motorised transport, with the remaining 35% being divided between non-motorised alternatives such as cycling and walking.
🚫 Proportion Language Pitfalls
Cars were the biggest piece of the pie. → Private cars accounted for the largest proportion.
38% was a very large number. → At 38%, private cars commanded the largest share.
Walking was 15%, which is small. → Walking constituted a relatively modest 15% of expenditure.
Cars increased. → ❌ WRONG TENSE — pie charts are STATIC. Never use increase/decrease unless comparing TWO charts.
⚡ Fraction Quick-Reference
100% = the entirety / the whole  |  ~75% = approximately three-quarters  |  ~66% = roughly two-thirds  |  ~50% = around half  |  ~33% = approximately one-third  |  ~25% = just over a quarter  |  ~10% = a mere one-tenth
Band 9 — Multi-Category in One Sentence
While private cars and public transport together accounted for nearly two-thirds of all expenditure at 65%, the remaining budget was shared almost evenly between cycling (20%) and walking (15%), each representing a comparatively minor proportion.

Section 3: Comparison & Grouping Structures

Analyse data analytically by grouping, contrasting, and ranking — the hallmarks of Band 7+.

🎯 Grouping is what separates Band 6 from Band 7+. Instead of listing every single figure one by one (boring), you group similar segments together and draw comparisons. This shows the examiner that you can think analytically, not just describe.
🔧 Comparison & Grouping Templates [Category A] was significantly larger than / dwarfed / was more than double [Category B].

[Category A] and [Category B] together accounted for / jointly represented / combined to make up [X%].

In contrast / By comparison / Conversely, [Category C] constituted a far smaller proportion at just [X%].
Band 6 — Basic Contrast
Private cars (38%) were much larger than walking (15%), which was the smallest category.
Band 7 — Analytical Grouping
When grouped together, motorised transport options — private cars and public transport — accounted for 65% of spending, considerably outweighing the combined share of non-motorised alternatives at just 35%.
Band 7 — Superlative Ranking
Private car use ranked highest, claiming the largest slice at 38%, followed by public transport (27%), cycling (20%), and walking (15%) in descending order.
Band 8 — Proportional Contrast
Private car expenditure, at 38%, was more than double that of cycling (20%) and nearly two and a half times the proportion attributed to walking — a stark contrast that highlights the overwhelming preference for private motorised travel.
Band 8 — Similar Categories
Cycling and walking showed comparable proportions, at 20% and 15% respectively, suggesting that non-motorised travel, while a minority overall, was relatively evenly distributed between these two options.
Band 9 — Complex Comparative Analysis
Although public transport and cycling differed by only 7 percentage points (27% versus 20%), the disparity between the two extreme categories — private cars and walking — was considerably wider at 23 percentage points, underscoring the dominance of car-based transport in this city.
🚫 Comparison Errors to Avoid
Cars are more bigger than walking. → Private car spending was significantly greater than the proportion attributed to walking.
Cars have 38% and walking has 15%, which is a difference of 23%. → Don't just calculate — interpret! Say what the difference means.
All the categories are different. → Too vague — specify how and by how much they differ.

🚀 Level Up: Grouping Strategy for High Scores

Instead of describing each slice one-by-one (Band 5 approach), create logical groups. For the transport example:


✅ Group 1: Motorised transport (Cars 38% + Public Transport 27% = 65%)

✅ Group 2: Non-motorised transport (Cycling 20% + Walking 15% = 35%)


This grouping strategy instantly makes your response feel analytical rather than descriptive — and that's exactly what Band 7+ examiners reward.

Section 4: Time & Sequence Expressions

Tense awareness and transitional flow — the structural glue of high-scoring responses.

🎯 Tense is the #1 mistake in pie chart responses. A single pie chart = simple past OR present tense (consistent!). Two pie charts comparing time periods = past for the earlier year, past for the later year, but with change language between them. Never mix tenses randomly.
⚡ Pie Chart Tense Rules
Single Pie Chart (no date): Use simple present — "Private cars account for..."
Single Pie Chart (past date, e.g. 2010): Use simple past — "Private cars accounted for..."
Two Pie Charts (comparing two years): Past for each year, change language between — "While cars accounted for 38% in 2000, this figure rose to 45% by 2020."
🔧 Sequence & Transition Templates Ordering: first and foremost / most notably / in particular / above all + [main point]

Addition: furthermore / additionally / in addition to this / equally noteworthy is + [extra point]

Contrast: in contrast / conversely / by comparison / on the other hand / whereas / while

Change between two charts: over the [X]-year period / between [year] and [year] / by [later year]
Band 6 — Simple Ordering
First, private cars accounted for 38% of spending. Then, public transport represented 27%. Finally, cycling and walking made up the rest.
Band 7 — Smooth Transitions
Most notably, private car use dominated at 38%. In second place, public transport followed at 27%, after which the proportions declined substantially, with cycling and walking trailing at 20% and 15% respectively.
Band 7 — Two Pie Charts (Temporal Comparison)
In 1990, housing costs represented 40% of household income; however, by 2020, this proportion had risen to 52%, making it an even more dominant category over the thirty-year period.
Band 8 — Precise Temporal Flow
Between 1990 and 2020, the most striking shift was observed in leisure spending, which expanded from a negligible share of 8% to 18% — more than double its original proportion — over the course of the period.
Band 8 — Contrast with "Whereas"
Whereas food and essentials comprised 30% of expenditure in 1990, this figure had contracted to 22% by 2020, reflecting a shift in consumer priorities over the decades.
🚫 Tense & Sequencing Mistakes
The pie chart shows that cars increased to 38%. → ❌ A single pie chart has NO change — remove "increased"!
In 1990, cars account for 40%. → In 1990, cars accounted for 40%. (past date = past tense)
First... Second... Third... Fourth... → ❌ Listing without grouping = Band 5. Use contrast and grouping language instead!

🚀 Level Up: Sequencing Within a Paragraph

Band 6 Approach (avoid this): "First, cars were 38%. Second, public transport was 27%. Third, cycling was 20%. Fourth, walking was 15%."

Band 8 Approach (do this): "Private cars commanded the largest share at 38%, with public transport in second place at 27%. The remaining 35% was split almost evenly between cycling and walking, at 20% and 15% respectively." — Notice: this covers all four segments in just TWO sentences using natural flow.

Section 5: Data Integration Techniques

Place specific numbers smoothly — not as a list, but as evidence woven into analysis.

🎯 Numbers are evidence, not content. The most common Band 5–6 mistake is writing responses that are just a list of percentages. High-scoring writers embed data as supporting evidence for a point they're making — like a lawyer using facts to prove a case, not just reading them aloud.
🔧 Data Embedding Frameworks [Analytical Point] + [, at X%,] + [further explanation]
e.g. Private cars dominated transport spending, at 38%, making them the single largest category.

[X% of / Approximately X% of] + [total] + [verb] + [subject]
e.g. Approximately 38% of total spending was directed towards private car use.

[Subject] + [verb] + [proportion phrase] + [(X%)]
e.g. Public transport accounted for just over a quarter (27%).
Band 6 — Data Listed
Cars were 38% and public transport was 27%.
Band 7 — Data Embedded
Private car use accounted for the largest proportion, at 38%, while public transport represented just over a quarter of all spending at 27%.
Band 7 — Parenthetical Data
The two motorised categories jointly commanded nearly two-thirds of the total budget, with private cars (38%) and public transport (27%) together accounting for 65% of all expenditure.
Band 8 — Data as Evidence of Point
The dominance of private vehicles in this city is clearly reflected in the data: car travel alone consumed 38% of the transport budget — a proportion that exceeds the combined total for cycling and walking (35%) by a small but notable margin.
Band 9 — Sophisticated Data Analysis
What is particularly striking is that private car use, despite representing only one of four categories, claimed 38% of the budget — a figure that alone exceeds the combined proportion of the two least-funded categories (35%) and surpasses by 11 percentage points the second-largest category, public transport (27%).
🚫 Data Integration Errors
38% is large. 27% is second. 20% is third. 15% is small. → ❌ This is NOT analysis — it's just reading numbers aloud.
Cars were 38.0% exactly. → Use natural approximations: "approximately 38%" or "just under two-fifths".
The percentage of cars is bigger. → Private car expenditure represented the dominant share at 38%.
⚡ Data Placement Rules
DO: Put the percentage after the subject and verb — "Cars accounted for 38%." (Not "38% was cars.")
DO: Use parentheses for quick data embedding — "...with cycling (20%) and walking (15%) making up the remainder."
DO: Round numbers for fraction language — "approximately one-fifth" sounds more sophisticated than "20.3%."
AVOID: Starting sentences with raw numbers — "38% was allocated to cars" feels mechanical.

✅ Pre-Submission Checklist — Band 7+ Guarantee

Before you finalise your pie chart response, tick every box below. If you can check all 12, you're writing at Band 7 minimum.

Introduction paraphrases the question WITHOUT copying exact wording.
Overview paragraph identifies the dominant category and one key contrast.
Tense is consistent and appropriate (past for past dates, present for undated charts).
I used at least 3 different proportion verbs (e.g., accounted for, comprised, represented).
I grouped similar segments together rather than listing all slices one by one.
Every percentage figure is embedded into a sentence, not listed as a bare number.
I used fraction language (e.g., "just over a quarter") alongside or instead of raw percentages.
I included contrast language (whereas, conversely, in contrast, while).
I used parenthetical data at least once: e.g., "cycling (20%) and walking (15%)".
My response does NOT include trend words like "increased/decreased" unless comparing two charts.
I used at least one complex sentence (compound or subordinate clause).
Response is between 150–200 words — concise but complete.
